When travel spend ran on informal approval and unrecorded expenses

Volcafe had no structured way for employees to request travel spend or for line managers to approve it with real visibility. This case study is the UX intervention: frame the broken as-is process, then design through high-fidelity wireframes for both roles.

01Design
strategy

The brief said “build a form”.
The problem was a broken accountability loop.

Employees travelling for client meetings, origin visits, and conferences were seeking travel-spend approval through WhatsApp and email. Line managers signed off without a structured view of what they were approving. Finance reconciled after the fact in Excel. The real problem was not “replace the spreadsheet” — it was employee initiative without a formal record, and manager oversight without data.

This work is a UX design case study through high-fidelity wireframes: diagnose the as-is spend-and-approval process, define the intervention, and specify screens for both roles. Visual UI, development, and launch metrics sit outside this scope.

Strategy frame

Executive Intent
  1. Give employees a formal path to request travel spend instead of informal chats
  2. Give line managers informed approval — not blind sign-off
  3. Connect proposed budgets to actual spend so finance can reconcile with context
Market Segment
  1. Business travellers (operations, origin country visits, conferences)
  2. Line managers reviewing high volumes of travel requests
  3. Finance processors closing trips against what was approved
General Tasks
  1. Create a proposed travel request (destination, dates, purpose, line items)
  2. Submit for approval and track status
  3. Capture receipts during or after travel
  4. Submit actual expenses against the approved proposal
  5. Approve or reject proposed spend, then actual spend
  6. See proposed vs actual variance before closing the trip
Tech Constraints
  1. Must be implementable in Microsoft Power Apps (web + mobile)
  2. Must sit in existing SharePoint, Teams, and Outlook infrastructure
  3. Multi-country, multi-currency travel
  4. This case study ends at high-fidelity wireframes — not a visual design system or production build
Trust & accountability goals Formal record, informed oversight, and an audit trail — so initiative is not punished and spend is not invisible.
Critical Success Factors
  1. An employee can request travel spend and know whether it is approved without chasing a manager
  2. An approver can see itemised proposed spend before signing off
  3. Proposed vs actual is visible on one screen at reconciliation
  4. Receipt capture does not require reconstructing a trip from a photo album weeks later

Design strategy artifact

The as-is tension is a scale, not a missing form: employees already plan travel; managers already “approve.” What is missing is a shared, recorded, informed loop.

Approach

I followed a user-centred process suited to an internal enterprise problem: start with the broken loop, not screens. Primary interviews and process artifacts (emails, WhatsApp threads, Excel files) showed that “approval” meant six different things, and that proposed spend and actual spend lived in separate worlds.

Two personas represented the widest gap in jobs-to-be-done: the employee who needs to move, and the line manager who is accountable for budget. I mapped both as-is journeys — request, informal approval, travel, reconstruction, finance closure — then designed one two-phase system that flexes for both roles, before moving into high-fidelity wireframes.

Key decisions

  1. 01
    Treat proposed and actual as one loop, not two tools
    Budget approval and expense reconciliation were disconnected. The intervention is a single request with two phases and a visible link between what was planned and what was spent.
  2. 02
    Two interfaces, same underlying request
    Employees need a guided submit-and-track flow. Approvers need scan, compare, and one-action approve/reject. Same data; different jobs.
  3. 03
    A shared definition of “approved”
    Verbal, WhatsApp, email, and finance stamp were all called approval. A six-stage lifecycle makes status explicit and non-skippable.
  4. 04
    Capture during the trip, not from memory
    Phone photos and faded memory were the real cost of “digital receipts.” Wireframes privilege adding expenses by date with attachments while the trip is still live.
  5. 05
    Show variance where the approver already looks
    Proposed vs actual belongs on the request card and the review screen — not only in a later Excel that finance types in blindly.
  6. 06
    Wire mobile at full parity
    Employees request and log spend while travelling; managers review on the go. High-fidelity wireframes cover desktop and mobile equally — not a stripped mobile-lite.
  7. 07
    Stop at high-fidelity wireframes
    This case study specifies structure, states, and flows. Colour systems, component libraries, and Power Apps build are the next phase — not claimed as finished UI here.

Assumptions made

  • Line managers are the primary approvers; finance processes after approval rather than replacing the manager.
  • Receipts in v1 are attached photos or files — no OCR or automatic extraction in this intervention.
  • English-first copy is sufficient for the wireframe set; localisation follows implementation.
  • Power Apps can support the two-phase lifecycle and role-based views; platform limits are treated as a later build risk, not as a reason to skip structure now.
  • One travel request equals one trip; multi-leg itineraries are still a single proposed/actual record.
  • Employees can save drafts; approval is only required to travel against a recorded budget, not to start filling the form.
02As-is
process

How employees seek approval —
and how approvers sign off blind

The current process looks “digital” because receipts live on phones and requests live in email. It is still broken: the employee reconstructs spend from memory; the line manager approves without a structured budget; finance types numbers with no trip context.

Employee: requesting travel spend

Employees already plan trips. What they lack is a recorded proposal. Approval is a message. Expenses happen in the world and then disappear into a camera roll until someone opens Excel.

Employee as-is — seeking approval
  • 1
    Plan the trip
    Dates, destination, purpose — often already decided before any budget is written down.
  • 2
    Ask the manager informally
    WhatsApp or email with a rough number. No itemised proposal. No shared status.
    Pain: “Approved” is a reply, not a record.
  • 3
    Travel and spend
    Physical slips or phone photos. Wallet and camera roll become the system of record.
    Pain: expenses occur; they are not captured against the trip.
  • 4
    Reconstruct, then email
    Hunt receipts, type Excel, attach scans. Average 3.4 hours, often weeks later.
Approver as-is — broken oversight
  • 1
    Receive an unstructured ask
    Inbox or chat. No queue, no totals, no comparison to other trips.
    Pain: no way to prioritise or scan pending spend.
  • 2
    Informal budget approval
    “OK” by message or verbal go-ahead. Trust substitutes for itemised review.
    Pain: sign-off is fast because it is uninformed.
  • 3
    Blind financial oversight
    No live view of approved vs outstanding. No proposed vs actual until Excel arrives.
  • 4
    Reconcile from memory
    Match a spreadsheet to a remembered chat. Finance then re-enters the same numbers without trip context.

Where the loop actually breaks

Managerial approval and finance processing barely overlap. The manager signs quickly; finance types blindly; the gap between them is errors and delay.

03Profile &
personas

Same trip.
Two completely different jobs to do.

Research across 18 interviews and 3 weeks of shadowing surfaced two primary personas with opposing mental models of what "a travel request" meant.

Persona A — The Employee
P. Sanjay
Operations Executive · Volcafe · India
Travels 4–8 times per year for business meets, origin visits, and conferences
Currently messages his manager on WhatsApp for travel approval before booking
Keeps expense receipts in a WhatsApp album and photos folder on his phone
Submits a final Excel with scanned receipts attached to email — often weeks after travel
Doesn't know if his request has been approved until he asks again
Submit the request and move on. Know that it's approved before booking. Not deal with it again until after the trip.
The post-trip reconciliation is the most dreaded part — reconstructing expenses from phone photos weeks later.
Persona B — The Line Manager / Approver
David Smith
Line Manager · Volcafe · United Kingdom
Reviews 30–50 travel requests per month across multiple countries and divisions
Currently approves via email reply — no structured view of pending requests
Has to reconcile proposed budgets against actual costs manually after travel
No visibility into whether approved trips have been reconciled or are still outstanding
Responsible to finance leadership for budget compliance — but has no live budget tracking
Review quickly, approve confidently, track outstanding reconciliations without chasing people.
The gap between what was proposed and what was actually spent is invisible until someone sends the final Excel — often months later.
"I approved the trip. I have no idea what was actually spent until I chase him for the Excel — and that's usually after he's already left for the next one."
— David Smith, Line Manager, during stakeholder interview
04Field
studies

Research before
a single wireframe

Distributed workforce across India, UK, and Europe required remote and in-person methods. The most valuable insight didn't come from interviews — it came from watching a user reconstruct a 3-week trip from 47 phone photos.

◎
Contextual Inquiry
Shadowed 8 employees during post-trip expense submission. Observed the actual tools used: WhatsApp, personal notes, SMS from hotels. Documented the average time-to-submit: 3.4 hours per trip.
Output → Behavioural patterns, tool audit
◈
Stakeholder Interviews
18 interviews across employees, approvers, finance officers, and HR. Key finding: approvers and employees had 6 different definitions of what "approved" meant in the current process.
Output → Mental model map, definition conflicts
◆
Process Audit
Collected 3 months of travel emails, WhatsApp screenshots, and Excel files from 5 employees. Mapped every step in the current process. Found an average of 14 touchpoints per travel request — most of them manual.
Output → As-is process map, friction count
◇
Competitive Audit
Reviewed SAP Concur, Expensify, Zoho Expense, and TravelPerk. Benchmarked the proposed-vs-actual two-phase model — none of the reviewed tools handled it well for multi-currency, multi-country enterprise use.
Output → Feature gap analysis, pattern library
◉
Power Apps Constraints Audit
Mapped what Power Apps could and couldn't support before locking wireframe patterns. Determined which flows needed simplification for implementation — treated as a design boundary for the wireframe set, not a post-handoff surprise.
Output → Technical constraint map, wireframe design boundaries
◐
Usability Testing (2 rounds)
Round 1: Paper flows with 6 employees and 3 approvers — validated the two-phase model and 6-stage lifecycle. Round 2: High-fidelity wireframe walkthroughs with 8 users, both personas, end-to-end request and approval paths.
Output → Task completion notes, navigation fixes for the wireframe set
05Data
gathered

Numbers that
changed the design direction

Four findings from research that each directly changed a design decision.

14
Manual touchpoints per travel request in the old process
WhatsApp message → verbal approval → email → Excel → scanned receipt → email attachment. 14 handoffs per trip, most of them invisible to finance.
3.4h
Average time to complete post-trip expense reconciliation
Employees spent 3.4 hours on average reconstructing expenses after travel. Most of that time was hunting for receipts — not filling in numbers.
6
Different definitions of "approved" across the organisation
Verbal, WhatsApp message, email reply, manager signature, finance stamp, and system flag. No shared definition. This directly drove the 6-stage lifecycle design.
100%
Of approvers had zero live visibility into outstanding reconciliations
Not a single approver could tell us how many trips they'd approved were still awaiting actual expense submission. The data existed — it was just completely invisible.
"I took the photos during the trip thinking I'd deal with them later. 'Later' was three weeks after I got back, and I couldn't remember what half of them were for."

06Task
analysis

To-be tasks —
what the intervention must support

Task decomposition for the designed intervention — not the as-is. The two-phase model is the core design insight: most tools treat expense submission as a single event. These tasks make the gap between proposed and actual a first-class concept in the wireframes.

Employee — P. Sanjay (Phase 1 + 2)
  • 1
    Create new travel request
    Fill destination, dates, purpose, opening balance medium (cash/card/both).
  • 2
    Add proposed expenses
    Category + amount + description per expense item. Save as Draft or Submit for Approval.
    Pain (old): Estimates were informal — no formal record before approval.
  • 3
    Track approval status
    Dashboard shows Pending Approval, Approved, Rejected, Action Required statuses.
  • 4
    Submit actual expenses (Phase 2)
    After travel — add expenses by date. Category, amount, currency, payment medium, attach receipts.
    Pain (old): Reconstructed from phone photos weeks after travel.
  • 5
    Submit actual expenses for approval
    Review total actual vs proposed. Submit. View proposed/actual/balance comparison.
  • 6
    Receive final approval or respond to rejection
    Edit and resubmit if rejected. Track closure.
Approver — David Smith (Phase 1 + 2)
  • 1
    Review incoming travel requests
    Card grid dashboard with status filter tabs: All, Pending Approval, Approved, Rejected.
    Pain (old): Email inbox with no prioritisation or status grouping.
  • 2
    Review proposed expenses
    Open request — see employee details, destination, dates, purpose, itemised expenses with total proposed.
  • 3
    Approve or reject Phase 1
    One-tap Approve or Reject. Status updated, employee notified.
  • 4
    Review actual expenses (Phase 2)
    See date-based expense breakdown. Proposed vs actual comparison with balance shown. Receipts attached and viewable.
  • 5
    Approve or reject actual expenses
    Final approval closes the request. Reject returns to employee for correction.
  • 6
    Track outstanding reconciliations
    Dashboard shows pending actual expense submissions. No more manual chasing.

Section 11
Reflection &
next steps
"The brief was a form. The work was a loop — and stopping at wireframes forced us to solve the loop before arguing about pixels."

The biggest lesson from scoping this as a UX case study through high-fidelity wireframes: the as-is artifacts mattered as much as the screens. The three infographics — employee vs manager tension, proposed vs actual chaos, and the reconciliation disconnect — did more to align stakeholders than any single dashboard layout.

If I ran the research again, I would balance approver participation earlier. Round 1 had 6 employee testers and only 2 approvers. The alternate approver card design — proposed and actual on the same card — came from that imbalance being corrected in round 2, not from the first wireframe pass.

What carries forward: full mobile parity in the wireframe set. Employees request and log spend while travelling; managers review on the go. Specifying both breakpoints at wireframe fidelity prevents a "mobile-lite" handoff later.

Out of scope for this case study: visual UI, Power Apps build, and launch validation. The natural next phase is implementation against the wireframes — with the platform constraint audit treated as a design input before development, not after.
